# Tipping in Vietnam: Etiquette, Rules & Amounts (2026)

Street stalls and luxury cruises follow completely different financial rules in Vietnam. At a family-run sidewalk shop serving **Phở (`45,000 VND`)**, **Bánh mì (`25,000 VND`)**, or **Cà phê sữa đá (`25,000 VND`)**, tipping (**Tiền tip** or **Tiền bồi dưỡng**) is neither expected nor practiced. Locals pay the exact price printed on the menu board and collect their small change to the last **5,000 VND** note.

Step into the international tourism sector, however, and expectations shift. For private tour guides, mountain drivers, Ninh Binh sampan rowers, Ha Long Bay cruise crews, and spa therapists, modest cash gratuities in **Vietnamese Dong (`VND`)** form an established, deeply appreciated supplement to base wages. Knowing exactly when to keep your wallet closed and when to hand over **50,000 to 250,000 VND** (`$2 to $10 USD` at `~25,400 VND = $1 USD`) removes awkwardness at every stop.

> **The Two-Handed Polymer Rule**: Always hand tips in **clean, untorn Vietnamese Dong (`VND`) polymer notes (`20,000`, `50,000`, `100,000`, or `200,000 VND`)** using **both hands (`hai tay`)** with a slight nod. Never tip with foreign coins (`$1`, `€1`, or `£1` coins cannot be exchanged at Vietnamese banks), and never leave **1,000 or 2,000 VND** paper notes (`$0.04 to $0.08 USD`) as a standalone tip, which reads as an insult rather than a thank-you.

## 1. Cultural Context and the 5% Service Charge Bill Rule

Traditional Vietnamese hospitality treats the quoted price as final. Sit on a low plastic stool in Hanoi's Old Quarter or District 1 in Ho Chi Minh City, and you are dining inside a family home. Relatives cook, serve, and clear tables. Leaving a spare **10,000 VND** bill behind on a stainless-steel noodle table confuses the vendor, who will frequently sprint half a block down the busy sidewalk to hand back your "forgotten" change.

Check your printed receipt carefully at mid-range bistros, craft breweries, rooftop lounges, and four- or five-star hotels. Upscale menus across Vietnam routinely list prices as **`++`**, which appends two separate lines to the bottom of your bill:

1. **`5% Phí phục vụ` (`Service Charge`)**: A mandatory 5% gratuity collected by the venue and distributed among floor, bar, and kitchen staff.
2. **`8% or 10% Thuế GTGT` (`Value Added Tax / VAT`)**: Government tax that goes to the state treasury, not to your server.

A **5% service charge** covers the tip. Still, rounding up your bill by **20,000 to 50,000 VND** ($0.80 to $2 USD) when a table server goes out of their way is always appreciated. At sit-down air-conditioned restaurants where no service charge is added, leaving **5% to 10%** in cash inside the bill folder is the local norm.

## 3. Field Protocols for Guides, Boats, Cruises, and Spas

### Tour Guides, Private Drivers, and Ha Giang Easy Riders

On a private full-day city or countryside tour (`8 hours`), tip the guide and the driver **separately**. Handing a combined lump sum to the guide and assuming it will be split creates confusion. A private English-speaking guide typically receives **150,000 to 250,000 VND per day** from a couple (`250,000 to 400,000 VND` from a family of four to six), while the driver who navigates highway traffic and keeps the van air-conditioned receives roughly half that amount (**100,000 to 150,000 VND per day**).

On the **Ha Giang Loop**, where an **Easy Rider** pilots you on the back of a 150cc motorcycle across **350 km** of steep mountain passes (**Ma Pi Leng** and **Tham Ma**) for 3 to 4 days, budget **200,000 to 300,000 VND per day** per driver—handing over **700,000 to 1,000,000 VND** ($28 to $40 USD) at the end of the loop. The same benchmark applies to local ethnic minority trekking guides (**Hmong, Red Dao, and Thai guides**) leading 12 to 18 km valley hikes through **Sapa's Muong Hoa Valley** or **Pu Luong Nature Reserve**: hand **150,000 to 200,000 VND per day** directly to your trail guide, plus **50,000 to 100,000 VND** to the local family hosting your village homestay dinner. On multi-day **Phong Nha-Ke Bang cave expeditions** (**Hang En** or **Tu Lan**), safety assistants and camp porters carry 20 kg gear packs over slippery limestone boulders; pooling **200,000 to 300,000 VND per trekker** at the trailhead debriefing is standard practice. On evening **Vespa or motorbike street-food tours** in Saigon, Hanoi, or Hoi An (`4 hours`), tipping your pillion rider **100,000 VND** ($4 USD) at hotel drop-off is customary.

### Boat Rowers in Ninh Binh and the Mekong Delta Plus Overnight Cruises

At **Trang An and Tam Coc** in Ninh Binh (`250,000 VND` admission ticket), local rowers—often older villagers rowing with their feet for **2 to 3 hours** across **6 to 12 km** of river caves—earn only a small fraction of the municipal ticket fee. A cash tip of **50,000 VND per passenger** (**100,000 VND per boat** for a couple) at the return pier is the fair local benchmark.

At **Tam Coc**, floating vendor boats near the turnaround point may ask if you want to buy a **50,000 VND** cold drink or snack for your rower. You are under zero obligation to buy overpriced river snacks; smile, say **"Không, cảm ơn"** (*No, thank you*), and give your **100,000 VND** cash tip directly to your rower back at the main dock so they keep 100% of the money. In the **Mekong Delta (`Ben Tre` or `My Tho`)**, where hand-rowed coconut-canal sampan rides last **15 to 20 minutes**, **20,000 to 50,000 VND per person** is ideal.

Aboard **Ha Long Bay and Lan Ha Bay overnight cruises**, dozens of behind-the-scenes workers—engineers, cooks, cabin stewards, and deckhands—never interact directly with guests. Drop **250,000 to 500,000 VND per cabin** (`$10 to $20 USD` for a 2-day/1-night sailing, or `500,000 to 850,000 VND` for 3 days/2 nights) into the **communal Tip Box at the reception desk** during checkout, and hand an extra **100,000 VND** directly to a butler or kayak guide who went above and beyond.

### Spas, Hotels, Restaurants, and Grab Ride-Hailing

For a **60- to 90-minute Vietnamese body massage, foot reflexology session, or herbal hair wash (`Gội đầu dưỡng sinh`, base price `250,000 to 500,000 VND`)**, therapists rely heavily on tips. Hand **50,000 to 100,000 VND** (`$2 to $4 USD`) directly to your therapist after dressing (`100,000 to 200,000 VND` at luxury hotel spas). Note that some budget tourist spas in Hanoi's Old Quarter and Hoi An print *"Tip Not Included — Minimum Tip 50,000–100,000 VND"* on their check-in cards.

In hotels, give bellhops **20,000 to 50,000 VND per heavy suitcase**, and leave **20,000 to 50,000 VND per night** on the bedroom pillow with a sticky note reading **"Cảm ơn"** (*Thank you*)—otherwise housekeeping staff will leave the bills untouched to avoid accusations of theft. For **GrabBike and GrabCar drivers**, tipping is completely optional (`0 VND` expected), though adding **10,000 to 20,000 VND** via the app during monsoon downpours or heavy airport bag loading is always appreciated.

## 4. Cash Etiquette and Polymer Note Rules

Vietnam's polymer banknotes start at **10,000 VND** (`~$0.40 USD`) and go up to **500,000 VND** (`~$19.70 USD`). Follow four simple cash habits to avoid awkward mistakes:

1. **Break `500,000 VND` Notes Early**: Excursion rowers, bellhops, and massage therapists rarely carry change for a **500,000 VND** bill. Break large notes at convenience stores (`Circle K`, `GS25`, `WinMart`) or hotel front desks each morning so your wallet always holds four or five **20,000, 50,000, and 100,000 VND** notes.
2. **Never Confuse the Two Blue Bills**: In dim evening lighting, the **20,000 VND note** (deep royal blue) and the **500,000 VND note** (cyan-greenish blue) look remarkably similar. Count the zeros twice so you do not accidentally hand over a `$20 USD` tip instead of `$0.80 USD`.
3. **Never Tip with `1,000` or `2,000 VND` Paper Notes**: Crinkled paper notes of **1,000, 2,000, or 5,000 VND** are used for motorbike parking tickets (`gửi xe`) and street change. Handing someone **2,000 VND** (`$0.08 USD`) as a tip is worse than giving nothing at all.
4. **Present Multi-Day Tips Discreetly**: For multi-day private guides or drivers, place the VND bills inside a small hotel envelope and offer it with **two hands (`hai tay`)** at the airport or station farewell. If you travel during the **Lunar New Year (`Tết Nguyên Đán`, late January or February)**, hospitality staff work through Vietnam's most important family holiday while many restaurants apply a temporary 15% to 20% holiday surcharge; slipping **50,000 or 100,000 VND** inside a red lucky-money envelope (**Phong bao lì xì**) with a smile and **"Chúc Mừng Năm Mới"** (*Happy New Year*) is one of the most respected cultural gestures a visitor can make.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I tip in US Dollars (USD) instead of Vietnamese Dong (VND)?
While cruise reception desks and multi-day private tour guides will accept **crisp, uncreased `$5`, `$10`, or `$20 USD` bills** (printed after 2013 with zero ink stamps or pinholes), local boat rowers, spa therapists, bellhops, and drivers strongly prefer **Vietnamese Dong (`VND`)**. Vietnamese banks reject slightly worn `$1` or `$5` bills and never exchange foreign coins, forcing workers to lose value at street money changers.

### What should I do if a Ninh Binh boat rower demands a larger tip mid-river?
Stay calm and polite. The vast majority of **Trang An** (strictly regulated by the UNESCO management board) and **Tam Coc** rowers are hardworking and gracious. If a rower pressures you mid-stream, smile, point toward the dock, and say **"Về bến gửi tiền cảm ơn"** (*I will give the thank-you tip back at the pier*). Waiting until the boat is docked ensures a smooth return paddle, and if serious misconduct occurs at Trang An, you can report the four-digit boat hull number to the pier supervisor.

### Do I need to tip on a free walking tour in Hanoi or Ho Chi Minh City?
Yes—depending on the structure. Volunteer student clubs (such as *Hanoi Kids* or *Saigon Hotpot*) strictly prohibit cash tips and only ask that you cover the student's **30,000 to 60,000 VND** coffee or street snack plus any temple entry tickets (`30,000 to 70,000 VND`). Commercial "free" walking tours booked via online portals operate on a tip-supported model, where **150,000 to 250,000 VND per person** (`$6 to $10 USD`) is the standard benchmark for a 2.5- to 3-hour walk.
