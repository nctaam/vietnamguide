# Hoi An Street Food Guide: 9 Essential Dishes & Stalls (2026)

Positioned along the alluvial banks of the Thu Bon River roughly 30 km (30 kilometers, 45 minutes by car at 45 km/h) south of Da Nang International Airport (DAD), the historic trading port of **Hoi An** preserves one of Southeast Asia's most geographically specific culinary traditions. Between the 15th and 19th centuries, wooden merchant junks from Fujian, Canton, Nagasaki, Amsterdam, and Macao anchored along the 1.2 km Bach Dang quay to trade silk, cinnamon, and ceramics.

That four-century maritime exchange created a hyper-local kitchen anchored to municipal soil and water chemistry. Signature dishes like **Cao Lầu** (35,000 VND to 50,000 VND / $1.40 to $2.00 USD) borrow textural cues from Japanese soba and Chinese char siu pork, yet rely strictly on alkaline water drawn from the 10 meters deep Cham-built Ba Le Well and wood-ash lye shipped 18 km (25 minutes by speedboat) from the offshore Cham Islands.

From wood-fired baguette ovens on Phan Chu Trinh Street baking 30,000 VND ($1.20 USD) loaves to riverside shacks on Cam Nam Island serving baby clams for 45,000 VND ($1.80 USD), this empirical 2026 guide details verified stall tariffs, walking distances, preparation mechanics, and food safety rules.

## The Sacred Dish: Cao Lầu and the Three Geographical Dependencies

To understand Hoi An's culinary architecture, start with **Cao Lầu**. Unlike pho or bun bo, authentic Cao Lau cannot be produced outside a 5 km radius of Hoi An Ancient Town due to three strict ingredient requirements.

First, the water used to soak the rice grain and knead the dough must be drawn from the **Ba Le Well** (*Giếng Bá Lễ*), a 10th-century square stone shaft built by the Cham people down a 1.5 meters wide alley off Tran Hung Dao Street (450 meters northeast of the riverside). Fed by a subterranean aquifer at 10 meters depth, the water remains at 22°C year-round with low iron and mild alkalinity, giving the noodle starch its firm elasticity.

Second, the soaking lye (*nước tro*) is boiled from the ash of melaleuca and eucalyptus firewood harvested on the offshore Cham Islands (`Cù Lao Chàm`), situated 18 km (25 minutes by canoe from Cua Dai Pier) off the coast. This mineral ash tints the 3 mm thick noodles a warm amber-yellow and creates a smoky, chewy bite after 20 minutes of steaming over wood fires.

Third, the fresh herb bed comes from the 300-year-old organic garden village of **Trà Quế**, located 3.0 km (10 minutes by bicycle) northeast of the Old Town along Hai Ba Trung Street. Tra Que farmers fertilize their 40-hectare sandy plots with freshwater lagoon seaweed rather than chemical manure, yielding pungent miniature basil (*húng quế*), fish mint (*diếp cá*), and baby mustard greens.

A finished bowl of Cao Lau combines 180 grams of warm noodles with 60 ml of reduced soy-and-five-spice pork roasting jus (never submerged in watery broth), 5 slices of braised pork loin (*thịt xá xíu*), blanched bean sprouts, Tra Que herbs, and square fried Cao Lau dough crackers (*tép mỡ*) plus a squeeze of calamansi lime and 1 teaspoon of Hoi An chili jam (*tương ớt Hội An*).

### Where to Eat Authentic Cao Lầu:
- **Cao Lầu Thanh (26 Thái Phiên Street, 600 meters north of the Old Town):** Open 07:00 to 19:00 (12 hours daily). A single-room neighborhood kitchen serving 300 bowls a day for 35,000 VND to 45,000 VND ($1.40 to $1.80 USD), with iced herbal tea at 5,000 VND ($0.20 USD).
- **Cao Lầu Bà Bé (Stall E007, Hoi An Central Market Food Hall, Tran Phu Street):** Open 06:30 to 14:30 (8 hours daily). Bowls cost 35,000 VND ($1.40 USD) and arrive within 2 minutes of sitting down on the stainless-steel counter stools.
- **Cao Lầu Không Gian Xanh (687 Hai Bà Trưng Street, 350 meters from Japanese Bridge):** Open 09:30 to 21:00 (11.5 hours). Serves smoky char siu bowls under timber rafters for 40,000 VND to 50,000 VND ($1.60 to $2.00 USD).

## The Great Baguette Rivalry: Bánh Mì Phượng vs Madam Khánh

Hoi An is the epicenter of the central Vietnamese **bánh mì**, anchored by two family bakeries located 550 meters (7 minutes walk) apart.

### Bánh Mì Phượng (2B Phan Châu Trinh Street)
Made famous globally by Anthony Bourdain in 2009, Bánh Mì Phượng runs a 6-person assembly line operating from 06:30 to 21:30 (15 hours daily). Each 22 cm loaf is custom-baked with pointed ends, a 1 mm paper-thin crust that shatters on bite, and a light interior crumb heated over charcoal embers for 90 seconds before serving.

The signature "Mixed Special" (*Bánh mì thập cẩm*, 35,000 VND to 45,000 VND / $1.40 to $1.80 USD) layers 12 distinct ingredients: rich pork liver pate, char siu pork slices, cinnamon pork sausage (*chả quế*), steamed pork roll (*chả lụa*), pork floss (*chà bông*), pickled green papaya and carrot ribbons, cucumber strips, Tra Que cilantro, spring onions, homemade egg mayonnaise, chili oil, and 2 tablespoons of warm pork-bone gravy.

### Madam Khánh - The Bánh Mì Queen (115 Trần Cao Vân Street)
Situated 450 meters north of Phan Chu Trinh Street, this shophouse was opened in the 1970s by Nguyen Thi Loc (born 1933), who personally oversaw the counter for over 45 years. Open 06:30 to 19:30 (13 hours daily), Madam Khánh charges 30,000 VND to 40,000 VND ($1.20 to $1.60 USD) per baguette, plus 15,000 VND ($0.60 USD) for fresh passionfruit or kumquat juice.

Madam Khánh's loaf leans slightly sweeter and richer. After toasting the baguette inside a 220°C electric deck oven for 2 minutes, staff spread thick liver pate, slow-braised five-spice pork belly, shredded chicken, a freshly pan-fried egg yolk, pickled radish, and a house-simmered tomato-chili-garlic glaze.

> [!NOTE]
> Takeaway queues at Bánh Mì Phượng frequently stretch 25 meters (a 15 minutes to 25 minutes wait) during peak lunch (11:30 to 13:30, 2 hours) and dinner (18:00 to 19:30, 1.5 hours). Visit between 07:00 and 09:30 or between 15:00 and 16:30 for a wait under 3 minutes. Madam Khánh seats 40 diners across two floors and rarely exceeds a 5 minutes wait.

## Exclusive Heritage: Cơm Gà, Mì Quảng & White Rose Dumplings

Beyond Cao Lau and baguettes, three foundational dishes define midday and early-evening dining across the ancient quarter.

### 1. Cơm Gà Hội An (Turmeric Shredded Chicken Rice)
Hoi An chicken rice uses free-range country hens (*gà kiến*, weighing 1.2 kg to 1.5 kg) poached for 35 minutes in ginger-and-turmeric stock. Long-grain rice from Quang Nam paddies is toasted with crushed garlic and chicken fat, then boiled in that golden broth over wood-fired stoves. The rice is plated with hand-torn chicken meat tossed in lime juice, Vietnamese coriander (*rau răm*), and thinly sliced onions, accompanied by a small bowl of chicken broth with giblets and 1 teaspoon of caramelized chili paste.
- **Cơm Gà Bà Buội (22 Phan Châu Trinh Street):** Founded in 1955; open 10:30 to 20:00 (9.5 hours). Plates cost 45,000 VND to 60,000 VND ($1.80 to $2.40 USD); whole shredded chicken salads cost 180,000 VND to 250,000 VND ($7.20 to $10.00 USD).
- **Cơm Gà Xí (47/2 Trần Hưng Đạo Street, 200 meters from Ba Le Well):** Open 11:00 to 19:30 (8.5 hours). Street-level plastic tables serving generous portions for 35,000 VND to 45,000 VND ($1.40 to $1.80 USD).

### 2. Mì Quảng (Quang-Style Turmeric Flat Noodles)
Originating in Quang Nam province, **Mì Quảng** features 8 mm wide rice noodles tinted yellow with fresh turmeric juice or left pure white, topped with braised river shrimp, pork belly, and whole quail eggs in 80 ml of concentrated peanut-oil and shrimp-head gravy. Every bowl is served with a 15 cm toasted black-sesame rice cracker (*bánh tráng mè*, 5,000 VND / $0.20 USD extra) that diners break directly into the bowl alongside sliced banana blossom and roasted peanuts.
- **Mì Quảng Ông Hai (6A Trương Minh Lượng Street, 700 meters east of Old Town):** Open 07:30 to 21:00 (13.5 hours); bowls cost 35,000 VND to 45,000 VND ($1.40 to $1.80 USD).
- **Mì Quảng Dì Hát (4 Phan Châu Trinh Street, street cart):** Open 17:30 to 22:00 (4.5 hours); bowls cost 30,000 VND to 40,000 VND ($1.20 to $1.60 USD).

### 3. Bánh Bao Bánh Vạc (White Rose Dumplings)
For over 90 years, the Tran family has held a near-monopoly on **Bánh Bao Bánh Vạc**, supplying 95% of Hoi An's restaurants from their central workshop at **White Rose Restaurant** (533 Hai Bà Trưng Street, 850 meters north of the Japanese Bridge, open 07:30 to 20:30). Twelve female artisans sit around a low circular table, pressing 15-times-filtered rice-and-tapioca dough into 3 cm translucent petals:
- **Bánh Bao (Round Rose):** Filled with 5 grams of minced river shrimp pounded with white pepper, scallions, and lemongrass.
- **Bánh Vạc (Crescent):** Filled with minced pork, wood-ear mushrooms, and bean sprouts.
Steamed for 6 minutes and topped with crispy shallots, a 15-piece platter costs 70,000 VND to 90,000 VND ($2.80 to $3.60 USD), paired with **Hoành Thánh Chiên** (crispy fried wonton squares topped with shrimp-tomato-pineapple salsa, 80,000 VND to 100,000 VND / $3.20 to $4.00 USD).

Crossing the 150 meters Cam Nam Bridge (`Cầu Cẩm Nam`), located 1.0 km east of the Central Market, leads onto **Cẩm Nam Island**. Along the first 400 meters of Nguyen Tri Phuong Street, 8 waterfront stilt shacks (including **Quán Bà Già** at 105 Nguyễn Tri Phương and **Quán Có Ngay** at 99 Nguyễn Tri Phương) specialize in **Bánh Đập Hến Xào**. Tiny freshwater clams (*hến*) raked from the 3 meters deep Thu Bon River bed are stir-fried over high heat for 3 minutes with crushed garlic, lemongrass, onion, peanuts, and Vietnamese mint (40,000 VND to 50,000 VND / $1.60 to $2.00 USD per plate). Diners scoop the clams onto **Bánh Đập** (a 20 cm sheet of wet steamed rice noodle pressed between two grilled sesame rice crackers, 15,000 VND to 20,000 VND / $0.60 to $0.80 USD) and dip each shard into fermented anchovy sauce (*mắm nêm*) spiked with pineapple and bird's-eye chili.

Back on the mainland, walk 5 minutes into the shaded lane beside the Ba Le Well to **Quán Giếng Bá Lễ** (45/51 Trần Hưng Đạo). For 110,000 VND to 130,000 VND ($4.40 to $5.20 USD) per person, staff bring a platter of charcoal-grilled pork skewers (*thịt nướng*), lemongrass pork sausages (*nem lụi*), crispy mini turmeric crepes (*bánh xèo*), and fried spring rolls (*ram cuốn*), which you wrap in dry rice paper with green mango, starfruit, and herbs before dipping into warm peanut-sesame pork liver sauce.

## Afternoon Snacks & Sweet Endings: Bánh Bèo, Xí Mà Phù & Chè Bắp

Between 14:00 and 18:00 (a 4 hours afternoon snack window when daytime temperatures average 26°C to 31°C), street corners fill with savory steamed rice cakes and sweet puddings.

At the intersection of Hoang Van Thu and Tran Phu streets (250 meters from the Japanese Bridge), **Bánh Bèo Bà Bảy** (02 Hoàng Văn Thụ) sets up low plastic stools from 13:30 to 18:00 (4.5 hours). Each order brings a tray of 4 to 6 miniature ceramic saucers holding steamed rice discs topped with brick-red wet shrimp-and-pork paste (*nhân tôm thịt*), crispy fried pork fat cubes, scallion oil, and a unique bamboo flat knife (*dao tre*) used to slice the cake inside its 5 cm dish (25,000 VND to 35,000 VND / $1.00 to $1.40 USD per tray).

For dessert, seek out **Xí Mà Phù** (Warm Black Sesame Sweet Soup) at **Quán Ông Thiều** (152 Nguyễn Trường Tộ, open 06:00 to 10:00, 4 hours) or from his daughter's evening street cart near the Ba Le Well (17:00 to 20:30, 3.5 hours). Introduced from Fujian over 100 years ago, this glossy black pudding simmers roasted black sesame, pennywort, Chinese medicinal root (*thục địa*), and sweet potato starch into a nutty tonic served in 100 ml porcelain bowls for 15,000 VND to 20,000 VND ($0.60 to $0.80 USD). Along Bach Dang riverside from 18:00 to 21:30, also sample **Chè Bắp Cẩm Nam** (sweet young corn pudding, 15,000 VND / $0.60 USD) and **Tào Phớ** (silky warm tofu pudding in ginger syrup, 15,000 VND / $0.60 USD) plus a glass of **Nước Mót** (lemongrass-lotus-cinnamon herbal tea at 150 Trần Phú, 18,000 VND / $0.72 USD).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What makes Cao Lầu different from other Vietnamese noodle soups?
Cao Lầu is served semi-dry with only 50 ml to 60 ml of concentrated soy-pork roasting jus rather than a full bowl of broth. Its 3 mm thick noodles require alkaline water from the 10 meters deep Cham-built Ba Le Well mixed with wood-ash lye from the Cham Islands (18 km offshore), creating a smoky, springy bite priced at 35,000 VND to 50,000 VND ($1.40 to $2.00 USD).

### Which is better: Bánh Mì Phượng or Madam Khánh?
Both cost 30,000 VND to 45,000 VND ($1.20 to $1.80 USD) and sit 550 meters (7 minutes walk) apart. Bánh Mì Phượng (2B Phan Châu Trinh) delivers a savory, herb-heavy profile with 5 types of pork, pate, and warm meat gravy on a razor-thin crust. Madam Khánh (115 Trần Cao Vân) offers a sweeter, caramelized tomato-chili glaze with braised pork belly and a pan-fried egg.

### Are street food stalls in Hoi An safe for travelers with sensitive stomachs?
Yes. Named family stalls like Cao Lầu Thanh, Cơm Gà Bà Buội, and Bánh Mì Phượng turn over 200 to 600 portions daily, meaning meats and herbs never sit stagnant in the 28°C to 33°C heat. Choose stalls where broth boils at 100°C, verify that iced drinks use cylindrical factory ice with a center hole, and wash your hands before unwrapping rice-paper rolls.

### How much should I budget for street food in Hoi An per day?
Budget 180,000 VND to 280,000 VND ($7.20 to $11.20 USD) per person per day for 4 full street meals and 2 drinks. That covers a morning bánh mì and coffee (55,000 VND / $2.20 USD), a midday bowl of Cao Lầu or Mì Quảng (40,000 VND / $1.60 USD), afternoon White Rose dumplings or baby clams (70,000 VND / $2.80 USD), and an evening Cơm Gà plus sweet soup (65,000 VND / $2.60 USD).

### How do I travel between Da Nang and Hoi An for food tours?
Hoi An sits 30 km south of Da Nang International Airport (DAD) along the coastal Vo Nguyen Giap / Lac Long Quan boulevard. A private 4-seat Grab car or taxi takes 40 minutes to 50 minutes and costs 300,000 VND to 380,000 VND ($12.00 to $15.20 USD) one-way, while shared shuttle minibuses charge 130,000 VND to 150,000 VND ($5.20 to $6.00 USD) per seat.
